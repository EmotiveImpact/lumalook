Why filters turn deep skin grey.

If you have deep skin, you've probably seen it: a filter that looks great on everyone else leaves your face flat, ashy or strangely orange. Your lighting isn't to blame. The cause is a few lines of maths, and they can be fixed.

Colour science23 November 20266 min readBy the Lumalook team

It starts with one slider

Almost every look adds contrast. Contrast picks a midpoint, then pushes everything brighter than it up and everything darker than it down. Where that midpoint sits decides who comes off worst.

Many photo tools apply contrast in what's called linear light, the raw maths of the sensor rather than the brightness your eye sees. In linear light, the midpoint lands at roughly 73% of the brightness you see on screen. Deep skin lives well below that line, so a look that feels gentle on light skin drags deep skin down hard.

73%where the midpoint really sits when contrast runs in linear light
1 in 3of the brightness range goes to pure black with a modest 20% contrast boost
Monk 9 and 10the deepest tones on the scale lost all detail in our tests

Why it goes grey, or orange

Skin that survives the crush still gets hurt in two ways.

Grey. Some looks lift the shadows by adding a little brightness to every pixel. Done in linear light, that lays a flat veil over the darkest tones. On the deepest skin it removed almost two thirds of the colour. That's the ashy look.

Orange. When contrast is applied to red, green and blue separately, blue runs out first in brown skin. What's left gets redder and more saturated. In our tests, Monk tones 7 and 8 gained around 80% more colour and drifted towards red. That's the orange cast.

An old problem in new code

None of this is new. For decades, film and photo labs were balanced against one reference face, and it was never a dark one. Kodak improved the browns in its film only after chocolate and furniture advertisers complained. We tell that story on our mission page. Today the bias lives in defaults: a midpoint here, an extra lift there, tuned on whoever was in front of the camera when the look was made.

We checked our own looks

Before launch we put every Lumalook look through the same test: 16 portraits of Black and brown people under four kinds of light, plus the full Monk chart. We weren't happy with what we found.

40of 133 looks kept skin true as they were
88needed a small fix to shadows, contrast or colour
5are being rebuilt from scratch

Our own recipe looks had exactly the linear contrast problem described above. So we're fixing it at the root, in the engine every look runs through, rather than patching looks one by one.

What we're changing

  • Contrast on lightness you can see. We apply contrast to perceived lightness (using the Oklab colour model), which leaves hue and colour exactly where they were.
  • A soft floor, not a crush. The deepest skin keeps its detail without being lifted to grey.
  • No grey veil. No looks that add flat brightness to the shadows.
  • Skin colour held steady. Through any look at its usual strength, skin hue moves less than five degrees and its colour stays within a narrow band.
  • The sheen stays. Highlights on skin are what make it look alive, so we protect them.
  • We never brighten faces for you. Rich and deep is the goal, not lighter.

Every look then goes back through the test, across all ten Monk tones, before it ships.

Help us check

Numbers only go so far. We're looking for creators to try Lumalook first, push it in the hardest light they can find and tell us where it still gets them wrong.
